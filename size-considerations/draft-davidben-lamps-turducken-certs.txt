                 Embedded Alternate Issuer Certificates
              draft-davidben-lamps-turducken-certs-latest

Abstract

   This document defines a mechanism to embed one or more alternate
   certificates within a single outer certificate.  The outer
   certificate remains parseable to a legacy relying party that is
   unaware of this specification.  Each alternate certificate certifies
   the same subject information, but with a different issuer.  This can
   be used to help TLS on the Web transition securely to post-quantum
   authentication, as well as other PKI transitions with similar
   constraints.

1.  Introduction

   In transitioning a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) to post-quantum
   cryptography, it is necessary to reconfigure relying parties to
   reject legacy trust anchors before cryptographically-relevant quantum
   computers (CRQCs) become readily available.  Otherwise, provisioning
   a service with a post-quantum certificate does not prevent downgrade
   attacks since an attacker equipped with a CRQC can attack any trust
   anchor that the relying party still trusts in order to forge a
   trusted certificate, not necessarily the one that issued the
   legitimate certificate.  The service's choice of CA does not impact
   this attack, only the relying party's trust anchors.

   Some applications, such as TLS on the Web, have constraints that make
   this particularly challenging:

   *  Upgraded relying parties must continue to connect to legacy
      services that do not yet support post-quantum algorithms.  Waiting
      for services to universally become post-quantum-capable will take
      time.

   *  Relying parties support enough services that out-of-band
      configuration of which services have migrated to post-quantum is
      impractical.

   *  Services must themselves continue to support legacy relying
      parties that do not support post-quantum algorithms or trust
      anchors.

   *  In addition to lacking post-quantum algorithms, some legacy
      services are also unable to configure and select between multiple
      certificates based on trust anchor or CA signature, which
      precludes a solution whereby services are automatically issued
      separate traditional and PQ-rooted-traditional certificate chains.

   [PQ-AUTH-ROADMAP] and [IETF126-PQ-AUTH] discuss how, for these
   applications, it is sufficient to migrate to a state where:

   1.  Legacy services serve certificates with post-quantum issuers,
       even if the end entity keys are still traditional.  This is known
       as a _post-quantum opt-out certificate_.

   2.  Upgraded relying parties only accept post-quantum issuers, but
       accept both post-quantum and traditional end-entity keys.

   A post-quantum opt-out certificate is a quantum-resistant proof that
   the service has authorized a traditional end entity key for upgraded
   relying parties.  It is expected that only services that are not yet
   post-quantum-capable will authorize such certificates.  A securely-
   configured post-quantum-capable service would authorize only its
   post-quantum key with post-quantum issuers, leaving its traditional
   key for traditional issuers and legacy relying parties.

   This issuer-side transition thus protects post-quantum services from
   downgrade, while remaining compatible with legacy services.  However,
   the final two constraints prevent one from simply replacing every
   legacy service's certificate on renewal, because the legacy services
   will lose compatibility with legacy relying parties who do not have,
   or are incapable of processing, a post-quantum certification path and
   trust anchor.

   This document solves this by providing a mechanism to embed the post-
   quantum opt-out certificate into a legacy certificate in a way that
   will be ignored by legacy clients unaware of this specification.
   This meets the security goals of delivering the post-quantum opt-out
   signal to updated relying parties, while still presenting a
   compatible certificate to unmodified legacy relying parties.  The
   embedding construction is also designed to meet two other
   requirements:

   *  It avoids increasing the size of logged precertificates in
      Certificate Transparency (CT), as described in Section 6.4.

   *  It aims to mitigate security risks of mismatched certificate
      contents between the outer and alternate certificates, as
      described in Section 7.2.

   While the Web PKI is the primary motivator, this mechanism may be
   appropriate for other PKIs with similar post-quantum migration
   constraints, as well as other similarly-constrained issuer-side PKI
   transitions.  In all cases, however, this is a temporary
   transitionary tool until services can deploy multiple certificates
   directly.  Section 6.1 discusses this in more detail.

2.1.  Terminology

   Subject:  The entity being described by a certificate.

   Issuer:  The CA that certified the information in a certificate.

   Outer certificate:  A certificate that embeds one or more alternate
      certificates using the mechanism defined in this document.

   Certification Authority (CA):  The (name, key) tuple representing a
      certificate issuer.

   Outer CA:  The CA that issues the outer certificate.

   Alternate certificate:  One of the certificates embedded in the outer
      certificate.  An alternate certificate certifies the same subject
      information as the outer certificate, but via a different issuer.

   Alternate CA:  A CA that issues one of the alternate certificates.

   Post-quantum opt-out certificate:  An end-entity certificate with a
      traditional subject key and post-quantum issuer.
      [PQ-AUTH-ROADMAP] embeds post-quantum opt-out certificates as
      alternate certificates in a fully traditional outer certificate.
      Other applications might issue and deploy them independently,
      without using this specification.

3.  Overview

   Outer and alternate certificates are closely related and issued in an
   integrated process:

   *  All certificates contain the same subject information, including
      subjectPublicKeyInfo and subjectAltName, but they differ in
      signature and issuer-specific information.  The common subject-
      specific extensions all share an ordering.

   *  The outer certificate embeds an AlternateIssuerInfo for each
      alternate certificate.  This contains both the signature and
      issuer-specific information for the alternate certificate.  It is
      sufficient to reconstruct the alternate certificate.

   *  Each alternate certificate contains an outer certificate binding
      extension.  This contains the issuer-specific fields of the outer
      certificate, but not the signature.  This authenticates the outer
      certificate's issuer-specific information in relying parties that
      only trust the alternate CA.

3.1.  Issuing Certificates

   After subject information is validated according to the policies of
   each CA involved, the certificates are issued in the following
   process:

   1.  The outer CA constructs an initial TBSCertificate with the
       validated subject information and issuer fields reflecting the
       outer CA.  It sets notBefore and notAfter such that the
       certificate validity would be compatible with each alternate CA.

   2.  For each alternate CA, run the following steps.  The steps for
       each alternate CA MAY run in parallel.

       a.  The alternate CA constructs a TBSCertificate based on the
           outer CA's initial TBSCertificate.  The outer CA's issuer-
           specific fields are removed and replaced with ones reflecting
           the alternate CA.

       b.  The alternate CA appends an outer certificate binding
           extension to its TBSCertificate, containing all fields
           removed or modified from the initial TBSCertificate, above.
           This authenticates the removed fields to a relying party that
           only trusts the alternate CA.

       c.  The alternate CA signs its TBSCertificate to issue an
           alternate certificate.  This TBSCertificate MUST satisfy the
           policies of the alternate CA.

       d.  The alternate CA extracts the alternate certificate's issuer-
           specific fields and signature.  It returns these to the outer
           CA as an AlternateIssuerInfo.  This contains enough
           information for the relying party to reconstruct the
           alternate certificate.

   3.  If the outer CA participates in CT, run the following steps.
       These steps MAY run in parallel with step 2.  If the outer CA
       does not participate in CT, skip these steps.

       a.  The outer CA signs a precertificate for its initial
           TBSCertificate, including the critical poison extension, as
           described in Section 3.1 of [RFC6962].

       b.  The outer CA then requests Signed Certificate Timestamps
           (SCTs) from CT logs.

   4.  The outer CA appends an id-ce-embeddedSCT-CTv1 (OID
       1.3.6.1.4.1.11129.2.4.2) extension to its initial TBSCertificate.
       The extension contains the AlternateIssuerInfo and, if
       applicable, SCTs collected above.

   5.  The outer CA signs its final TBSCertificate to issue the outer
       certificate.  This TBSCertificate MUST satisfy the policies of
       the outer CA.

   Figure 1 illustrates this process.  Although the two issuance
   processes are now integrated, from the outer CA's perspective, it is
   analogous to embedding SCTs in CT.  From the alternate CA's
   perspective, this is issuing a certificate with a specific extension
   added.

   While these are two distinct CAs, their policies and certificate
   issuance have to be sufficiently aligned to issue these closely
   related certificates.  Often the two CAs will be operated by the same
   entity.

   Section 4 discusses certificate issuance in more detail.

3.2.  Verifying Certificates

   When the certificate subject presents the outer certificate to a
   relying party, relying parties will attempt to validate one of
   several certificates:

   *  Relying parties that are not aware of this protocol will ignore
      the unrecognized SCT type of alternate_issuer_info and validate
      the outer certificate as usual.  Section 6.2 discusses this
      further.

   *  Upgraded relying parties will reconstruct the alternate
      certificates and use them as candidate target certificates when
      building certification paths [RFC4158].  If a relying party finds
      some valid certification path from some target certificate, outer
      or alternate, it proceeds with that certification path.  This
      specification does not impose a preference for whether outer or
      alternate is used in a case where both have a valid certification
      path.

   This allows the same certificate to serve both older relying parties
   that expect the outer CA, and newer relying parties that expect one
   of the alternate CAs.

   Section 5 discusses this in more detail.

4.  Certification Authorities

4.1.  Certificate Constraints

   Although outer and alternate certificates are conceptually separate,
   with relying parties accepting one or the other, they are constrained
   to both share subject information and incorporate parts of each
   other.  Alternate certificates are incorporated into the outer
   certificate to transmit them.  The outer certificate is also
   partially incorporated into the alternate certificate to mitigate a
   class of security risks in relying parties.  These are described in
   more detail in Section 7.2.

   This means outer and alternate CAs are expected to coordinate their
   certificate profiles and issuance processes, as outlined in
   Section 3.1.  Concretely, the certificates are constrained as
   follows:

   The outer and alternate certificates MUST have a version of v3 and
   MUST omit issuerUniqueID and subjectUniqueID.  These are preexisting
   requirements from Sections 4.1.2.1 and 4.1.2.8 of [RFC5280].

   The outer and alternate certificates MUST have the same validity,
   subject, and subjectPublicKeyInfo values.

   The outer and alternate certificates' extensions lists MUST each
   consist of, in order, zero or more _common extensions_, zero or more
   _issuer-specific extensions_, and a single _final extension_. These
   MUST satisfy the following:

   *  The common extensions across all certificates MUST be equal and
      appear in the same order.

   *  The issuer-specific extensions MUST NOT contain any of the
      following extensions:

      -  id-ce-subjectKeyIdentifier, Section 4.2.1.2 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-keyUsage, Section 4.2.1.3 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-subjectAltName, Section 4.2.1.6 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-subjectDirectoryAttributes, Section 4.2.1.8 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-basicConstraints, Section 4.2.1.9 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-nameConstraints, Section 4.2.1.10 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-extKeyUsage, Section 4.2.1.12 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-pe-subjectInfoAccess, Section 4.2.2.2 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-CABFOrganizationIdentifier, [CABF-EV]

   *  PKI deployments MAY impose additional restrictions on issuer-
      specific extensions by policy.  In particular, deployments SHOULD
      forbid extensions which would change the certificate's subject
      information in ways which would risk the issues described in
      Section 7.2.  The following is a non-exhaustive list of extensions
      which would be safe to permit as issuer-specific in most
      applications:

      -  id-ce-authorityKeyIdentifier, Section 4.2.1.1 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-certificatePolicies, Section 4.2.1.4 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-issuerAltName, Section 4.2.1.7 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-cRLDistributionPoints, Section 4.2.1.13 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-ce-freshestCRL, Section 4.2.1.15 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-pe-authorityInfoAccess, Section 4.2.2.1 of [RFC5280]

      -  id-pe-qcStatements, Section 3.2.6 of [RFC3739]

   *  The outer certificate's final extension MUST have type id-ce-
      embeddedSCT-CTv1, with contents as described in Section 4.2.  This
      embeds each alternate certificate in the outer certificate.

   *  Each alternate certificate's final extension MUST have type id-pe-
      outerCertificateBinding, with contents as described in
      Section 4.3.  This authenticates most of the outer certificate
      from the alternate certificate.

4.2.  Alternate Certificate Information

   Each alternate certificate is described as differences from the outer
   certificate in an AlternateIssuerInfo structure.  An
   AlternateIssuerInfo is defined as follows:

   AlternateIssuerInfo ::= SEQUENCE {
       serialNumber         CertificateSerialNumber,
       signatureAlgorithm   AlgorithmIdentifier{SIGNATURE-ALGORITHM,
                                {SignatureAlgorithms}},
       issuer               Name,
       commonExtensionCount INTEGER (0..MAX),
       issuerExtensions     SEQUENCE OF Extension{{CertExtensions}},
       signatureValue       BIT STRING
   }

   An AlternateIssuerInfo's fields are defined as follows:

   *  serialNumber is the alternate certificate's serial number.

   *  signatureAlgorithm is the alternate certificate's signature
      algorithm.  This value appears in both the TBSCertificate and
      Certificate structures.

   *  issuer is the alternate certificate's issuer name.

   *  commonExtensionCount is the number of common extensions, as
      defined in Section 4.1.  This value MUST be strictly less than the
      number of extensions in the outer certificate, to exclude the
      outer certificate's final extension.

   *  issuerExtensions is the alternate certificate's issuer-specific
      extensions, as defined in Section 4.1.

   *  signatureValue is the alternate certificate's signature value.
      This is a signature over the reconstructed TBSCertificate, not the
      AlternateIssuerInfo.

4.2.1.  Signed Certificate Timestamp Encoding

   The AlternateIssuerInfos for each alternate certificate are embedded
   in the outer certificate.  Instead of a custom extension, this
   document embeds each AlternateIssuerInfo as a new type of SCT in the
   outer certificate's id-ce-embeddedSCT-CTv1 (OID
   1.3.6.1.4.1.11129.2.4.2) extension.  This extension MUST be the final
   extension in the outer certificate.  Section 6.4 discusses the
   motivation for embedding the alternate certificates as SCTs.

   The contents of this extension are defined in Section 3.3 of
   [RFC6962], but the definition is replicated here for clarity.  The
   extnValue OCTET STRING field itself contains a DER-encoded OCTET
   STRING.  This OCTET STRING's contents are
   SignedCertificateTimestampList defined using the TLS presentation
   language below.  Note there are two layers of OCTET STRING encoding.

   opaque SerializedSCT<1..2^16-1>;

   struct {
       SerializedSCT sct_list<1..2^16-1>;
   } SignedCertificateTimestampList;

   Each AlternateIssuerInfo is formated as a TransItem structure
   (Section 4.5 of [RFC9162]) with a versioned_type of
   alternate_issuer_info, defined below:

   enum {
       alternate_issuer_info(TBD3), (0xFFFF)
   } VersionedTransType;

   struct {
       VersionedTransType versioned_type;
       select (versioned_type) {
           case alternate_issuer_info:
               opaque contents<1..2^16-1>;
       } data;
   } TransItem;

   The contents field contains the DER encoding of the
   AlternateIssuerInfo.

   The serialized TransItem is then placed in the
   SignedCertificateTimestampList as an opaque SerializedSCT.  The
   SignedCertificateTimestampList MAY contain other SCT constructions,
   notably the SignedCertificateTimestamp structure defined in
   Section 3.2 of [RFC6962] if the outer CA participates in CT.  Per
   Section 6.2, relying parties are expected to ignore unrecognized SCT
   versions.

   Note: [RFC6962] was obsoleted by [RFC9162], but the two protocols use
   separate extensions.  AlternateIssuerInfos are embedded in the
   extension from [RFC6962] for compatibility with existing deployments.
   They extend the structure from [RFC9162] to make use of the registry
   defined in Section 10.2.3 of [RFC9162].  This can be embedded in the
   extension from [RFC6962] because the two-byte VersionedTransType type
   reserves 0x0000..0x00FF to avoid overlapping with
   SignedCertificateTimestamp's one-byte v1(0) version value.
   Allocating new values from VersionedTransType preserves this
   separation.

4.3.  Outer Certificate Binding

   Each alternate certificate contains an outer certificate binding
   extension, defined below:

   OuterCertificateBinding ::= SEQUENCE {
       serialNumber         CertificateSerialNumber,
       signatureAlgorithm   AlgorithmIdentifier{SIGNATURE-ALGORITHM,
                               {SignatureAlgorithms}},
       issuer               Name,
       commonExtensionCount INTEGER (0..MAX),
       issuerExtensions     SEQUENCE OF Extension{{CertExtensions}}
   }

   id-pe-outerCertificateBinding OBJECT IDENTIFIER ::= {
       iso(1) identified-organization(3) dod(6) internet(1) security(5)
       mechanisms(5) pkix(7) pe(1) TBD2 }

   ext-outerCertificateBinding EXTENSION ::= {
       SYNTAX OuterCertificateBinding
       IDENTIFIED BY id-pe-outerCertificateBinding
       CRITICALITY FALSE
   }

   The OuterCertificateBinding structure is similar to the
   AlternateIssuerInfo, except that it does not contain a signatureValue
   field.  It describes the outer certificate, relative to the alternate
   certificate.

   *  serialNumber is the outer certificate's serial number.

   *  signatureAlgorithm is the outer certificate's signature algorithm.
      This value appears in both the TBSCertificate and Certificate
      structures.

   *  issuer is the outer certificate's issuer name.

   *  commonExtensionCount is the number of common extensions, as
      defined in Section 4.1.  This value MUST be strictly less than the
      number of extensions in the alternate certificate, to exclude the
      outer certificate binding extension itself.

   *  issuerExtensions is the outer certificate's issuer-specific
      extensions, as defined in Section 4.1.

   The outer certificate binding extension MUST be the final extension
   in the alternate certificate and MUST be non-critical.  It contains
   enough information to reconstruct the outer CA's initial
   TBSCertificate (see Section 3.1).  CAs MUST NOT issue an outer
   certificate binding extension if this reconstructed TBSCertificate
   violates the constraints in Section 4.1, or is otherwise inconsistent
   with applicable policies.  In particular, the issuerExtensions field
   MUST NOT contain any issuer-specific extensions forbidden by
   Section 4.1 or applicable policies.

   This binding mitigates the security risks described in Section 7.2 by
   authenticating most of the replaced outer certificate by the
   alternate CA.

   Note that the design of this mechanism precludes nested alternate
   certificates since both the outer certificate's id-ce-embeddedSCT-
   CTv1 extension, and the alternate certificate's id-pe-
   outerCertificateBinding extension must be last, thereby precluding a
   certificate from simultaneously being both an outer and alternate
   certificate.  This does not preclude the alternate certificate from
   embedding SCTs because [RFC6962] does not have an ordering
   requirement.

5.  Relying Parties

   Relying parties process alternate certificates as an extension of
   X.509 path building [RFC4158].

   While collecting candidate certification paths, a relying party
   checks if the outer certificate's final extension is id-ce-
   embeddedSCT-CTv1.  If so, it looks for SCTs formatted as in
   Section 4.2.1.  For each such SCT, it decodes the AlternateIssuerInfo
   structure.  For each successfully-decoded AlternateIssuerInfo, the
   relying party then reconstructs the alternate certificate as
   described in Section 5.1.

   If successful, the alternate certificate is used as an alternate
   target certificate.  The relying party then considers certification
   paths that end at the alternate certificate.  If certification path
   validation (Section 6 of [RFC5280]) succeeds for some path, the
   relying party proceeds with this path, as if it had found a valid
   path ending at the outer certificate.

   Certification paths for outer and alternate certificates are
   processed independently.  Relying parties MAY extract and consider
   alternate certificates regardless of the trust, revocation, or expiry
   status of the outer certificate.

   An outer certificate with many AlternateIssuerInfo structures can
   require more processing time in the relying party.  This is similar
   to path building itself [RFC4158], X.509 policy validation [RFC9618],
   certification path length, and other factors that contribute to X.509
   scaling.  Relying parties SHOULD limit the number of alternate
   certificates in a single outer certificate they will process.  For
   example, if the relying party has a path building iteration limit,
   considering an alternate certificate can contribute to that limit.
   An attacker can inject arbitrarily many AlternateIssuerInfo
   structures into an unverified outer certificate, so this avoids a
   denial of service vector.

5.1.  Reconstructing the Alternate Certificate

   The following procedure reconstructs an alternate certificate from
   the outer certificate and an AlternateIssuerInfo.

   1.   Check each of the following.  If any check fails, abort this
        procedure.

        *  The outer certificate's version is v3.

        *  The outer certificate's signatureAlgorithm and its
           TBSCertificate's signature fields are equal.

        *  The outer certificate has no subjectUniqueID or
           issuerUniqueID field.

        *  The outer certificate has at least commonExtensionCount + 1
           extensions and the final extension is id-ce-embeddedSCT-CTv1.

   2.   Make a copy of the outer certificate as a Certificate structure.

   3.   Replace the TBSCertificate's serialNumber field with the
        AlternateIssuerInfo's serialNumber.  Let outerSerialNumber be
        the replaced value.

   4.   Replace the Certificate's signatureAlgorithm field and the
        TBSCertificate's signature field with the AlternateIssuerInfo's
        signatureAlgorithm.  Let outerSignatureAlgorithm be the replaced
        value.

   5.   Replace the TBSCertificate's issuer field with the
        AlternateIssuerInfo's issuer.  Let outerIssuer be the replaced
        value.

   6.   Remove the TBSCertificate's final extension.  The processing
        steps in Section 5 ensure this extension has type id-ce-
        embeddedSCT-CTv1 and contains the AlternateIssuerInfo, formatted
        as described in Section 4.2.1.

   7.   Remove all but the first commonExtensionCount extensions in the
        TBSCertificate.  Let outerIssuerExtensions be the removed
        extensions.

   8.   Check outerIssuerExtensions and the AlternateIssuerInfo's
        issuerExtensions as described in Section 5.2.  If rejected,
        abort this procedure.

   9.   Append the AlternateIssuerInfo's issuerExtensions to the
        TBSCertificate.

   10.  Append a non-critical outer certificate binding extension
        (Section 4.3) to the TBSCertificate such that:

        *  serialNumber is outerSerialNumber.

        *  signatureAlgorithm is outerSignatureAlgorithm.

        *  issuer is outerIssuer.

        *  commonExtensionCount is the AlternateIssuerInfo's
           commonExtensionCount value.

        *  issuerExtensions is outerIssuerExtensions.

   11.  Replace the Certificate's signatureValue field with the
        AlternateIssuerInfo's signatureValue.

5.2.  Checking Issuer-Specific Extensions

   Section 4.1 aims to forbid outerIssuerExtensions and issuerExtensions
   above from containing any extensions that would change the
   certificate's subject information.  However, X.509 is extensible, so
   a relying party cannot enforce this restriction in full.  In
   particular, relying parties MUST accept unrecognized issuer-specific
   extensions in this procedure.  (If the unrecognized issuer-specific
   extension is critical, it will likely be rejected later during path
   validation.)

   The relying party SHOULD check for and reject the forbidden issuer-
   specific extensions listed in Section 4.1 and other issuer-specific
   extensions known to be forbidden by local policy.  The relying party
   MAY skip this check for any extension that its path verifier does not
   recognize.  It also MAY skip this check if all subsequent access
   checks reliably inspect the correct certificate, as described in
   Section 7.2.

5.3.  Input Certificates and Validated Certificates

   For this section, we define the input certificate to be the entire
   certificate being processed (typically the outer certificate), and
   the validated certificate to be the one for which the certification
   path was checked, which may be the outer certificate or an alternate
   certificate.  Note that the input certificate will always contain the
   validated target certificate, but does not specify which one it is.
   This section considers potential impacts of the input certificate and
   the validated certificate being different.

   In contexts that are defined based on the certificate that was sent
   over the wire, the input certificate is used.  For example:

   *  The TLS transcript hash (Section 4.1 of [RFC9846]) is computed
      from the Certificate message and thus the bytes of the input
      certificate.

   *  The tls-server-end-point channel binding (Section 4 of [RFC5929])
      is computed from the bytes of the input certificate.

   In contexts that are defined based on the certificate that was
   validated, notably access control checks, the validated certificate
   SHOULD be used.  Section 7.2 discusses this case in more detail.

   This distinction is a generalization of an existing distinction in
   path-building X.509 applications.  The input list of certificates may
   differ from the final certification path that was built.
   Applications are already expected to separate these cases, but they
   were not previously required to consider different target
   certificates.

6.  Deployment Considerations

6.1.  Legacy Services

   This document defines a temporary transitionary mechanism for
   deployments where some legacy services do not support multiple
   certificates.  Services that support multiple certificates directly
   SHOULD NOT use this mechanism.  Deployments are RECOMMENDED to
   enforce this in CAs and relying parties.

   For example, in a post-quantum transition for TLS, this mechanism is
   only applicable to unmodified legacy services that only have
   traditional end-entity keys.  Once a service has a post-quantum end-
   entity key, it can be assumed to be relatively up-to-date and
   necessarily maintains separate end-entity keys for both legacy and
   post-quantum relying parties.  The service also necessarily has
   sufficient certificate selection capabilities (Section 4.5.1.2 of
   [RFC9846]) to select between its supported relying parties.  Such a
   service would have no need for this document.  Accordingly, in a TLS
   deployment using this mechanism for a post-quantum transition:

   *  CAs SHOULD NOT embed alternate certificates if the end-entity key
      is post-quantum.

   *  Relying parties SHOULD ignore alternate certificates if the end-
      entity key is post-quantum.

   This ensures that, when the post-quantum transition is completed,
   services will no longer rely on this mechanism and implementations
   can remove the supporting logic.

   A given deployment SHOULD only deploy this mechanism once, for only
   one transition.  After that transition has completed, the deployment
   SHOULD have transitioned to a state where certificate holders support
   multiple certificates.

6.2.  Legacy Relying Parties

   The primary backwards compatibility goal of this specification is
   that a relying party that is unaware of this specification will
   process the outer certificate as if the alternate was not present.
   This assumes that existing certificate parsers will ignore
   unrecognized SCTs of type alternate_issuer_info and continue
   processing the rest of the certificate as usual.

   This is believed to be the behavior of all existing parsers, and is
   supported by both [RFC6962] and [RFC9162]:

   Section 3.3 of [RFC6962] says:

      This encoding ensures that TLS clients can decode each SCT
      individually (i.e., if there is a version upgrade, out-of-date
      clients can still parse old SCTs while skipping over new SCTs
      whose versions they don't understand).

   Section 6.3 of [RFC9162] says:

      This encoding ensures that TLS clients can decode each TransItem
      individually (so, for example, if there is a version upgrade, out-
      of-date clients can still parse old TransItem structures while
      skipping over new TransItem structures whose versions they don't
      understand).

   A PKI deployment can verify broad compatibility by experimentally
   deploying an AlternateIssuerInfo in some widely-accessed test
   certificate.  For example, this test certificate could be served from
   a background request on a widely-accessed webpage.  This
   AlternateIssuerInfo MAY be a fixed placeholder value if no alternate
   CA is yet available, provided the issuer field does not match the
   name of a real CA.  Relying parties will ignore alternate
   certificates issued by CAs they do not trust, so this placeholder
   will not impact relying parties that implement this document.

   Such experiments could miss more specialized non-compliant relying
   parties that only talks to a few services.  Should such a relying
   party exist, a PKI deployment can stil use this mechanism if they
   first prioritize upgrading those services to support multiple
   certificates directly.  This document is only needed for legacy,
   single-certificate-only services.

6.3.  Certificate Sizes

   Embedding alternate certificates into an outer certificate increases
   the overall certificate size.  In particular, embedding a post-
   quantum opt-out certificate adds large post-quantum signatures to the
   otherwise traditional outer certificate.

   While Merkle Tree Certificates [I-D.ietf-plants-merkle-tree-certs]
   aims to decrease post-quantum certificate sizes with landmark-
   relative certificates, this optimization is not available here.  This
   mechanism targets legacy, single-certificate-only services, so the
   alternate certificate needs to be standalone for compatibility with
   all supporting relying parties.  This means:

   *  Legacy services connecting to post-quantum relying parties serve
      standalone certificates, when landmark-relative certificates might
      have been possible.

   *  Legacy services connecting to legacy relying parties serve extra
      post-quantum signatures, when legacy relying parties ignores them
      anyway.

   This size increase could increase latency for those connections.
   However, the impact is limited to legacy services.  Any given service
   can mitigate the latency cost by upgrading to be fully post-quantum-
   capable and multi-certificate-capable.  This gives optimal behavior
   for the service:

   *  Upgraded services connecting to post-quantum relying parties can
      select between landmark-relative and standalone certificates.

   *  Upgraded services connecting to legacy relying parties can serve
      traditional certificates, with no extra certificates embedded.

   A PKI can thus mitigate this size cost in two steps: First, upgrade
   widely-accessed, faster-moving services to post-quantum.  Then,
   deploy embedded post-quantum opt-out certificates to any remaining
   services that have not yet upgraded.  The size costs will be limited
   to those legacy services.  As legacy services then upgrade, they
   simultaneously become quantum-resistant and decrease certificate
   sizes.

6.4.  Certificate Transparency Log Sizes

   In deployments that use CT [RFC6962], a direct post-quantum
   transition risks significantly increasing the sizes of CT logs.
   [IETF124-PLANTS] estimated an 8.3x size increase from migrating to
   ML-DSA-44.

   Merkle Tree Certificates [I-D.ietf-plants-merkle-tree-certs] provide
   a quantum-resistant transparency construction that avoids this size
   increase.  However, when post-quantum Merkle Tree Certificates are
   embedded as alternate certificates, the resulting large
   AlternateIssuerInfo structures will instead be logged with the outer
   certificate.  The traditional CT logs would increase in size during
   the transition to Merkle Tree Certificates.

   Section 4.2.1 partially mitigates this new increase by embedding
   AlternateIssuerInfo structures in SCTs.  When the outer certificate's
   precertificate is logged, the precertificate will omit these SCTs and
   thus the added structures.

   This is only a partial mitigation because the AlternateIssuerInfo
   will still be logged when the final certificate with embedded SCTs is
   logged.  Deployments using this document to transition away from a
   CT-based PKI thus SHOULD permit CT logs to reject logging of any
   certificates with embedded SCTs of type alternate_issuer_info.  This
   will not impact other embedded SCTs, which refer to logged
   precertificates rather than logged final certificates.

7.  Security Considerations

7.1.  Separate Certificates

   Although each alternate certificate is ultimately encoded relative to
   the outer certificate, the alternate CA's signature is on the
   reconstructed alternate TBSCertificate, so it is still considered to
   have separately issued the reconstructed alternate certificate.  In
   particular:

   *  The alternate TBSCertificate MUST satisfy the policies of the
      alternate CA, including any domain validation.  Misissuance of an
      alternate certificate remains misissuance.

   *  Revocation of each certificate is independent.  Revoking the outer
      certificate does not revoke the alternate certificates and vice
      versa.

   When relying parties evaluate candidate certification paths, they
   likewise treat each possible target certificate separately.  If the
   relying party accepts an alternate certificate, it does not verify
   the outer certificate or even necessarily trust the outer CA.  In
   particular, in the post-quantum transition, the outer CA is a
   traditional CA, so its key could be compromised by a CRQC.

7.2.  Certificate Mismatch

   Access checks in the relying party SHOULD act on the certification
   path that was ultimately validated, failing to do so can result in
   access control bypass vulnerabilities.  In support of this, APIs that
   return the validated certification path SHOULD reflect the correct
   target certificate.

   However, this breaks an application invariant.  Previously, the
   target certificate would never change as a result of path building.
   Which trust anchor it chains to and via which path might change, but
   the end entity certificate would not.  An existing application might
   assume this invariant and inspect the original target certificate for
   some access check.  If the application's X.509 implementation is
   updated independently from the rest of the application, it may
   inadvertently use the wrong certificate, which could carry security
   implications if the content of the alternate and outer certificate
   differ.  In some cases, the X.509 implementation's APIs might not
   even return the validated path, only a success or failure status.

   X.509 implementations MAY further mitigate the risk by limiting
   alternate certificate support to applications that opt in.  However,
   this might undesirably slow down PKI transitions that depend on
   alternate certificates.  Additionally, a large application might opt
   in, yet forget to update some component.

   This document further mitigates this risk in two ways:

   1.  Where possible, limiting alternate certificates to encoding the
       same subject information as the outer certificate.

   2.  Requiring the alternate CA to sign over removed or replaced outer
       certificate fields, via the outer certificate binding extension.

   This contrasts with [I-D.bonnell-lamps-chameleon-certs] and
   [I-D.ietf-lamps-certdiscovery], which allow the second certificate to
   differ significantly.  There, the application is expected to be fully
   aware of the second certificate.

   If a more general protocol were instead used in this document, path
   validation might, for example, authenticate an alternate
   certificate's public key, while the rest of the application acts on a
   different, unverified public key in the outer certificate.  This
   could allow an attacker to bypass certificate-based authentication.
   However, alternate certificates cannot replace the
   subjectPublicKeyInfo field in this document, so a public key mismatch
   is not possible.

   X.509 extensions complicate this mitigation.  X.509 does not
   structurally distinguish between subject and issuer extensions, in a
   way that would allow a relying party to classify unrecognized
   extension types.  If the relying party rejects unknown issuer-
   specific extensions, it is not possible to define new such
   extensions.  However, if it accepts unknown extensions, it could
   allow an extension that downstream components depend on being the
   same between the alternate and outer certificate.

   Instead, the outer certificate binding extension mitigates this risk
   more generally.  Except for id-ce-embeddedSCT-CTv1, extensions
   replaced or removed from the outer certificate are still signed by
   the alternate CA.  This prevents an arbitrary attacker from
   manipulating those fields.  Section 4.3 additionally holds the
   alternate CA responsible for meeting the requirements in Section 4.1
   and other deployment-specific policies, to forbid the alternate CA
   itself from enabling an attack.  CAs are expected to not sign unknown
   extensions, so this does not conflict with extensibility.

   Ultimately, the alternate CA is responsible for ensuring that any
   extensions removed or modified will not cause interoperability or
   security problems among the target relying party base.

   However, there are some cases where this is complicated by relying-
   party-imposed constraints on the CA.  If the alternate CA were under,
   say, a name constraint (Section 4.2.1.10 of [RFC5280]), the relying
   party could not rely on the alternate CA alone to mitigate
   subjectAltName mismatches.  If the alternate certificate contained a
   permitted subjectAltName, while the outer certificate contained an
   excluded one, a mismatch could bypass the name constraint.
   Section 5.2 recommends that the relying party check replaced
   extensions in a more limited way.  Though the relying party might not
   recognize all extensions, its path validator necessarily recognizes
   all extensions that it incorporates into path validation.
